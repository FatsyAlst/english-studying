## 📖 Definition
### Sense 1 — *adverbial phrase / idiom* (Most Common)
Used to indicate that one is summarizing a longer story or explanation; cutting to the essential point. Full form: *to make a long story short* (American) or *to cut a long story short* (British).

> [!example] Examples
> - Long story short, we missed the flight and had to stay overnight.
> - To make a long story short, they broke up and she moved to another city.
> - So, long story short, I ended up getting the job after all.

## 📜 Etymology
The expression has been in use since at least the 19th century. Some attribute its origin to Henry David Thoreau, who wrote "Not that the story need be long, but it will take a long while to make it short" (1857). The idea of abbreviating a narrative is ancient — similar expressions exist in Latin (*ne longum sit*, "lest it be long") and other languages.

---
## 💡 Usage Notes
> [!tip] Notes
> - Almost always placed at the beginning of a sentence or clause, signalling that the speaker is skipping details.
> - American English prefers *"to make a long story short"*; British English prefers *"to cut a long story short."*
> - Very conversational — rarely used in formal writing.
> - Often preceded by *"so"* or *"anyway"*: *"So, long story short..."*
