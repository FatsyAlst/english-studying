## Shall vs Should

**Should** started life as the past tense of **shall**, so they look like a present/past pair. In modern English they have split into two different modal verbs. The only place they really overlap is in questions with *I* and *we*, and even there they mean different things.

> [!tip] The core idea
> **Shall I / shall we…?** = an **offer** or a **suggestion** (*Do you want me to…? / Let's…?*)
> **Should I / should we…?** = asking for **advice** (*Is it a good idea to…?*)

---

### Shall

**Shall** is now used almost only with **I** and **we**, and its use depends on the context:

1. **Offers**: *"**Shall** I open the window?"* (= Do you want me to?)
2. **Suggestions**: *"**Shall** we go to the cinema tonight?"* (= Let's go?)
3. **Asking for a decision**: *"What **shall** we do this weekend?"* / *"Where **shall** I put this?"*
4. **Question tag after *let's***: *"Let's take a break, **shall we**?"*
5. **Formal or old-fashioned future**: *"This time next week I **shall** be in Scotland."* Most people now just say *I'll* or *I will*.
6. **Rules, contracts and laws**, where it means *must*: *"Candidates **shall** remain in their seats until all papers have been collected."*
7. **Promises and determination** (literary and solemn): *"You **shall** go to the ball!"*, *"They **shall** not pass."*

Negative: **shall not → shan't** (/ʃɑːnt/ in British English). It is distinctly British: *"I shan't be long."* In North America it is rare.

> [!example] Typical usage
> - *Shall I make us a cup of tea?*
> - *Shall we meet at the station at seven?*
> - *It's getting late. Let's head home, shall we?*
> - *The tenant shall pay the rent on the first day of each month.*

---

### Should

**Should** works with **every** person (*I, you, he, they…*) and has a broad range of meanings:

1. **Advice / the right thing to do**: *"You **should** see a doctor."* / *"You **shouldn't** drink and drive."*
2. **Asking for advice**: *"**Should** I call him and apologise?"*
3. **Expectation / probability**: *"The bus **should** arrive at five."* / *"The roads **should** be quieter today."*
4. **Something expected that didn't happen** (*should have* + past participle): *"The bus **should have** arrived ten minutes ago."*
5. **Regret or criticism about the past**: *"I **should have** studied more."* / *"You **shouldn't have** said that."*
6. **Formal "if" (a possible event)**: *"If you **should** change your mind, let me know."* → even more formal with inversion: *"**Should** anyone call, tell them I'm busy."*
7. **Reported speech (past of shall)**: *"Shall I come at eight?"* → *"He asked if he **should** come at eight."*

**British-only (and mostly formal) uses**, which you'll meet in UK writing and speech:

8. **After *that* with suggest / recommend / insist / important**: *"She recommended that I **should** take some time off."* American English normally drops *should* here: *"…that I take some time off."*
9. **After adjectives of feeling**: *"It's strange that he **should** say that."*
10. **Polite requests and wishes with I/we** (= *would*): *"I **should like** to make a complaint."* / *"We **should be** grateful for your help."*
11. **Tentative opinions**: *"I **should think** it'll take about three hours."* / *"I **should imagine** so."*
12. **Strong agreement**: *"I **should hope** so!"* / *"I **should think** not!"*

> [!example] Typical usage
> - *You should try the route at least once before race day.*
> - *The results should be online by Friday.*
> - *I should've brought an umbrella. Belgian weather, again.*
> - *Should you require any further information, please contact the faculty office.*

### Key Differences

| Aspect | Shall | Should |
|--------|-------|--------|
| **Main job** | Offers, suggestions, (formal) future, legal obligation | Advice, expectation, regret, formal conditions |
| **Subjects** | Almost only *I* / *we* (except in rules and laws) | Any subject |
| **Question meaning** | *Shall I help?* = Do you want me to? | *Should I help?* = Is it a good idea? |
| **Negative** | *shan't* (British, rather formal) | *shouldn't* (everywhere, very common) |
| **Past form** | — (its old past tense *is* **should**) | *should have* + past participle |
| **Register** | Formal / British in most uses | Neutral, everyday |
| **Frequency** | *Rare and declining* | *Very common* |
| **Pronunciation** | /ʃæl/, weak /ʃəl/ | /ʃʊd/, weak /ʃəd/ (the **l** is silent, like *could* and *would*) |

---

### British vs American English

**Shall** is clearly more British. Oxford Learner's Dictionaries notes that in British English *shall* is still used with *I* and *we* in questions and for suggestions and offers, but it is used far less in North American English. There, people say:

- *Should I open the window?* or *Do you want me to open the window?* instead of *Shall I…?*
- *Want to go?* / *Let's go!* instead of *Shall we go?*
- *I won't be long.* instead of *I shan't be long.*

**Should** is common in both varieties, but British English uses it more because of the extra formal uses above (*I should like…*, *…recommended that he should…*).

The numbers back this up. Geoffrey Leech's corpus study compared matched one-million-word samples of written English from 1961 and 1991/92:

| Modal | British 1961 | British 1991 | Change | American 1961 | American 1992 | Change |
|-------|--------------|--------------|--------|----------------|----------------|--------|
| **shall** | 355 | 200 | −43.7% | 267 | 150 | −43.8% |
| **should** | 1301 | 1147 | −11.8% | 910 | 787 | −13.5% |

Two takeaways:
1. **Shall** dropped by almost half in only 30 years, on both sides of the Atlantic.
2. British English uses **both** words more than American English, and has done since at least 1961.

> [!info] Legal "shall" is fading too
> Contracts and laws have traditionally used *shall* for obligations. Many plain-English drafters, in the UK and the US, now prefer **must**, because *shall* can mean *must*, *will*, *may* or just *is*, depending on the context. If a document mixes *shall*, *will* and *must*, a court may assume they mean different things.

### The Old Rule (that nobody follows anymore)

Traditional grammar (going back to John Wallis in 1653) had a complicated rule:

- **Plain future**: *I / we **shall***, but *you / he / they **will***.
- **Determination or command**: the **reverse**: *I / we **will***, but *you / he / they **shall***.

A classic grammar joke shows the rule at work: a foreigner falls into a river and shouts *"I **will** drown, and no one **shall** save me!"* By the old rule this means *"I'm determined to drown and I forbid anyone to help me!"*, so the English bystanders politely let him drown. He should have shouted *"I **shall** drown, and no one **will** save me!"*

Today, this distinction has almost disappeared. Use *will / 'll* for the future, and keep *shall* for offers and suggestions.

### The Portuguese Trap

Portuguese has one verb, **dever**, where English splits the meanings across several words:

| Portuguese | English |
|------------|---------|
| *Quer que eu abra a janela? / Posso abrir a janela?* | **Shall** I open the window? |
| *Vamos ao cinema?* | **Shall** we go to the cinema? |
| *Devo ligar para ele? / Será que eu ligo?* | **Should** I call him? |
| *Você deveria descansar.* | You **should** rest. |
| *Devo pagar até sexta.* (obrigação) | I **must** / **have to** pay by Friday. |

The trap is translating **"Shall I…?"** as **"Devo…?"**. It isn't asking whether you are obliged to do something, or whether it's a good idea. It's offering to do it. If you mean *"Is it a good idea?"*, use **should**.

> [!info]- 🇧🇪 Bonus: the Dutch connection
> *Shall* comes from Old English *sceal* (infinitive *sculan*), whose original meaning was **"I owe, I am obliged to"**. Its Germanic cousins kept different halves of that meaning:
> - **Dutch *zullen*** kept the **future / offer** side, exactly like English *shall*: *"**Zal** ik het raam openen?"* = *"**Shall** I open the window?"*; *"**Zullen** we gaan?"* = *"**Shall** we go?"*
> - **German *sollen*** kept the **obligation / advice** side, closer to *should*: *"Du **sollst**…"* = *"You **should** / **are to**…"*
>
> So the next time someone in Leuven says *"Zullen we…?"*, you know it's the same word as English *"Shall we…?"*

### Common Mistakes

- ❌ *Shall you come tomorrow?* → ✅ *Will you come tomorrow?* / *Are you coming tomorrow?* (modern *shall* questions only take *I* / *we*)
- ❌ *You should to study.* → ✅ *You should study.* (modals take the bare infinitive, no *to*)
- ❌ *I should of called.* → ✅ *I should've called.* (*should've* sounds like "should of", but it's *should have*)
- ❌ *She shoulds…* → ✅ *She should…* (modals never take *-s*)
- ❌ *Should I help you with your bags?* (when you mean it as an offer) → ✅ *Shall I help you with your bags?*

> [!tip] The Shortcut
> **Shall I…?** = *I'm offering.*
> **Shall we…?** = *I'm suggesting.*
> **Should I…?** = *I'm asking for your opinion.*
> Outside questions with *I* / *we*, you almost always want **should** (advice) or **will** (future).

### Quick-Fire Practice

Choose **shall** or **should**:

1. *It's cold in here. \___ I close the window?*
2. *You \___ really see a doctor about that cough.*
3. *Let's start the meeting, \___ we?*
4. *I'm not sure what to wear to the interview. \___ I wear a suit?*
5. *The train \___ arrive at platform 3 in five minutes.*
6. *\___ you need any help, just give me a call.*
7. *The buyer \___ pay the full amount within 30 days.* (contract)
8. *"\___ I bring anything?" → She asked if she \___ bring anything.*

> [!tip]- Answers
> 1. **Shall**: an offer (*Do you want me to?*)
> 2. **should**: advice
> 3. **shall**: tag question after *let's*
> 4. **Should**: asking for advice (*Is it a good idea?*)
> 5. **should**: expectation / probability
> 6. **Should**: formal *if* with inversion (= *If you need any help…*)
> 7. **shall**: legal obligation (modern plain English: **must**)
> 8. **Shall** → **should**: *should* is the past of *shall* in reported speech
